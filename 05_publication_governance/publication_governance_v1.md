# What This Stream Tests

Two candidate claims from the register:

- **C4** — Hyperauthorship combined with collaboration-internal pre-review makes external peer
  review structurally incapable of auditing a result.
- **C5** — Strategy and feasibility panels are composed substantially of prospective
  beneficiaries, so machine-justification documents cannot be read as independent assessment.

The frame document sets the falsification conditions. The publication critique fails if there
is a working mechanism by which an outside specialist can audit a multi-thousand-author result
and force a correction. The governance critique fails if strategy bodies include members with no
prospective stake, and if their recommendations have historically gone against the interest of
their own membership.

Both tests were run. The publication critique survives in a narrower and more specific form than
stated. The governance critique partly fails on the first condition and survives on the second,
but what survives is a claim about a *composition rule*, not about the integrity of the people
appointed under it. One topic assigned to this stream — suppression of null results — turned out
to be the reverse of a weakness, and is recorded as such.

# Hyperauthorship: The Arithmetic of the Byline

The record holder is a joint ATLAS–CMS Higgs mass measurement, *Phys. Rev. Lett.* 114, 191803
(2015), with 5,154 authors [A]. The paper runs 33 pages; the first nine carry the research and
references, and the remaining 24 are the author list and institutional affiliations (1). The
measurement itself fixed the Higgs mass to ±0.25% (2). The 2012 ATLAS Higgs discovery paper is 38
pages, 14 of them author list (4).

ATLAS has about 6,000 members, of whom roughly 3,000 are scientific authors, drawn from over 200
institutes in 40 countries [A] (7). As of early 2021, CMS had passed 1,000 publications and ATLAS
stood at 964; all LHC experiments together had produced 2,852 journal papers, plus 380 papers
written by individuals on behalf of collaborations and 10,879 non-journal items [A] (2).

The term *hyperauthorship* is Blaise Cronin's, from a 2001 JASIST paper [A] (53). The best
quantitative comparison available is a 2004–2013 study of four journals, two in high-energy
physics and two in genetics, covering 8,693 articles [A] (3):

| Measure | High-energy physics | Genetics |
|---|---|---|
| Articles sampled | 4,679 | 4,014 |
| Articles with 100+ authors | 419 (8.9%) | 88 (2.3%) |
| Author range in those articles | 101–3,171 | 100–437 |
| Mean authors, hyperauthored articles | 764.6 | 170.4 |
| Mean authors, all articles | 73.1 | 20.7 |
| Mean authors per article, 2004 → 2013 | 23.2 → 152.1 | 11.5 → 35.5 |
| Mean institutions per hyperauthored article | 782.3 | 224.6 |

HEP author lists are alphabetical, so the Higgs discovery paper is cited as "G. Aad *et al.*"
[A]. Aad was a junior physicist whose alphabetical position made him nominal lead author until
M. Aaboud displaced him in 2016 (4). Author order therefore carries no information about
contribution. The same study notes that, because HEP's collective alphabetical convention is
read as a claim of equal contribution, the HEP journals sampled did not require author
contribution statements, while *Nature Genetics* did [A] (3). No major collaboration publishes
per-author CRediT roles; individual contributions are recorded internally in analysis notes,
service records and conveners' reviews [A] (8).

# Authorship as Membership Right, Not Contribution Claim

The operative rules are published and specific. ATLAS requires that a prospective author have
been a qualifying member for at least one year, not be an author on another major LHC
collaboration, and complete "a bare minimum of 80 eight-hour working days" of pre-agreed
technical work, logged in the Glance database [A] (6). The work must be concentrated on the
agreed topic; the rules state that spreading the time thinly does not count. Authorship then
runs for as long as the member's employment contract does, and converts to twelve months of
sign-in-only status afterwards. Gaps under one month are handled automatically; one to six months
are normally approved without justification; beyond twelve months the person must re-qualify,
with no exceptions.

The consequence is that authorship attaches to *standing membership*, not to the specific paper
[A]. A member who meets the criteria appears on every paper published during their qualifying
membership, whether or not they worked on that result (8). Spencer Klein, a senior scientist at
Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory writing in *Retraction Watch*, states it plainly:
"Everyone is an author on every paper, whether or not their specialized work contributed to that
paper," and there is no requirement that an individual author have read the paper or helped write
it [A] (5). Papers are drafted by a small team; individual members typically get one opportunity
to comment before submission. Klein notes that the Fermi LAT collaboration requires members to
opt in to each paper's author list, and doubts most members read the papers they are listed on.

The ATLAS Authorship Committee's published responsibilities are implementing authorship policy,
maintaining the authorship database and managing author lists. Responsibility for paper content
is not among them [A] (6).

This produces a specific gap, and it is worth naming precisely rather than loosely. Cronin
described the link between authorship and accountability as part of the "standard model" of
scholarly publishing; hyperauthorship severs it in both directions, making it hard to locate
responsibility for an error and hard for an early-career researcher to demonstrate individual
credit [A] (4). Nobel recognition went to two people in 2013 and three in 2017 for results whose
author lists ran to thousands (4). The 2004–2013 bibliometric study puts the mechanism in one
line: "Inappropriate multiple authorship leads to dilution of authorship responsibility and
unjustified citations" [A] (3).

A fair counterweight: the Snowmass career-pipeline report records that some large experiments
charge an institutional entry fee *and* an annual fee for authorship, and recommends that entry
fees scale with the number of PhD holders at an institution [A] (42). Authorship in these
collaborations is therefore partly a purchased institutional good, which strengthens the
"membership right" reading rather than the "contribution claim" reading.

# What Happens Inside the Collaboration Before a Journal Sees the Paper

The internal process is elaborate, documented, and in the specific cases on record it works. Any
honest account of C4 has to start there.

CMS, as described through its 1,000th paper [A] (9):

1. The team pitches the plan to the responsible CMS subgroup and gets approval to proceed.
2. Preliminary analysis; in this case the first approach lacked sensitivity and was rebuilt,
   including a new analysis tool that itself had to pass CMS quality control. That rework took
   nine months.
3. Several rounds of feedback from group conveners and an *external* Analysis Review Committee,
   ending in approval to unblind the data.
4. The team answers the ARC's questions.
5. Collaboration-Wide Review: every CMS member is notified and may comment — "potentially
   hundreds of scientists." The team responded to every comment and revised the paper to correct
   a misleading interpretation that outside readers had flagged.
6. Final reading, then submission to the journal by the CMS Publications Committee, whose chair
   reviews every paper before submission.

Total analysis time about three years; the review stages roughly four months, plus about a month
for the final reading.

ATLAS runs a three-phase PAPER workflow [A] (10). Phase 0 forms the Analysis Team, names
conveners, constitutes an Editorial Board appointed by the Publications Committee chair, and
produces a first draft. Phase 1 validates and circulates the draft to the collaboration and holds
an approval meeting, requiring sign-off from the PubComm chair and the Spokesperson. Phase 2 is a
second circulation and closure meeting with final sign-off. The Editorial Board enforces ATLAS
policies, reviews the analysis, and decides whether it is worth publishing at all. Results go
through internal review and a collaboration-wide paper approval meeting before release (7).

Junk and Lyons, writing in *Harvard Data Science Review*, add two features that matter for the
accountability question [A] (11). Statistics committees — typically at least six members —
advise on systematic uncertainties. And collaborators who disagree with a result can have their
names removed from the author list. Blind analysis is standard: selections are optimised on
simulated data or control samples before the signal region is opened, precisely to stop
conscious or unconscious tuning toward a desired answer, which otherwise biases p-values low
and signal strengths high.

The field's defence here is strong and should be stated at full strength: this machinery exists
*because* external review cannot scale to a 3,000-person analysis, and it is more rigorous than
what a two-referee journal review would supply in any field.

# What External Peer Review Can Actually Audit

The question C4 asks is what is left for the journal to do. The field's own answer is on the
record, and it is the strongest single piece of evidence in this stream.

The LHC Reinterpretation Forum report — *SciPost Phys.* 9, 022 (2020), roughly 130 authors from
across the experimental and phenomenology communities — is an inventory of what published LHC
results *lack* that would be needed to use them independently [A] (12):

- Full likelihoods and search-region correlations are supplied only in some cases; ATLAS does not
  usually give search-region correlations. At the time of writing, ATLAS had published full
  likelihoods in JSON format for **two** analyses.
- Efficiency maps for all signal regions are given in "very few cases."
- Background estimates are "frequently, but not universally, published electronically," and are
  usually not decomposed by process.
- Detector efficiency and resolution data are often not in digitised or executable form.
- Analysis pseudocode is "relatively rare," and the frameworks needed to run executable analysis
  code are often not public.
- Generator inputs — SLHA files, run cards, UFO model files, Monte Carlo samples — are often
  missing or minimal.
- Theory-parametrisation changes often require refits to raw data that is "often not provided (as
  it can be seen to be too sensitive to release)."
- Machine-learning-based analyses "cannot be reproduced at all outside the experimental
  collaboration."
- Where phenomenologists have reimplemented analyses from published pseudocode, "significant
  errors have also been discovered."
- The report states that the process for publishing numerical data "too often fails to complete
  in general purpose experiments."

Junk and Lyons concur from the other direction: published results often lack full correlated-
uncertainty information, which prevents exact reproduction; external reproduction faces barriers
in complexity, support and collaboration approval; and open-data efforts remain in testing and
"need a cultural change" [A] (11). They also state that collaborations can fall into
"group-think."

Against this, the only published editorial reflection found on refereeing a mega-collaboration
paper is thin. For the 5,154-author paper, *PRL* editor Robert Garisto described the main
challenge as merging two author lists with "slightly different styles," and praised the teams'
handling of referee and editorial comments [A] (1). Accountability for content is not discussed.

# Null Results: Where the Critique Fails

The brief asked whether exclusion limits are published as thoroughly as discoveries, and whether
HEP is unusually good at this. It is, and the standard critique of publication bias does not
apply. This is recorded as a Tier D rejection below, but the evidence belongs here.

Fanelli's 2012 cross-disciplinary study of 4,656 papers across 20 disciplines found overall
positive-result rates rising from 70.2% in 1990–91 to 85.9% in 2007 [A] (13). Space science had
the *lowest* overall frequency of positive results and was one of only two disciplines showing a
slight decline over time. Physics sat at an odds ratio of about 1.93 relative to space science —
joint 12th–13th of 20, below materials science (3.16), psychology/psychiatry (2.99), immunology
(2.77) and economics/business (2.65). The physical sciences cluster at the bottom of the
positive-results league table, which is where a field without a file drawer should sit.

The structural reason is visible in the output. ATLAS and CMS have published over 2,000 papers
between them, the overwhelming majority of which are searches and measurements reporting
agreement with the Standard Model. ATLAS's own Run 2 exotics review describes search after
search where "no significant excess is seen," with the largest deviation discussed at about 3.5σ
local and about 1.5σ global [A] (14). Exclusion contours are the standard deliverable, published
in their own right, with summary plots aggregating them. A null search in HEP is not an
unpublishable result; it is the expected result and the normal product.

Note what this does *not* fix. Publishing an exclusion limit is not the same as publishing the
information needed to re-derive it — the Reinterpretation Forum report concerns exactly the
second. HEP publishes its nulls thoroughly and publishes the means of checking them
incompletely. These are separate properties, and conflating them would be the error the frame
document warns against.

# arXiv: Gatekeeping at the Preprint Layer

arXiv's moderation system is run by volunteer subject-matter experts with terminal degrees,
approved by discipline-level advisory committees and arXiv staff [A] (15). Moderation is
explicitly not peer review, and moderators give no feedback. Published grounds for declining a
submission include poor form, unprofessional tone, insufficient preparation, and — the broad one
— "lack of originality, novelty, or significance." Authors may appeal either a classification or
a decision to decline; arXiv may require journal acceptance before considering an appeal; and
"decisions upon appeal are final," with no feedback given. Moderators keep the review process
confidential. No statistics on rejection or removal rates are published.

The endorsement layer tightened in January 2026 [A] (16). An institutional email address alone no
longer qualifies a new submitter to a category. First-time submitters must either hold an
academic or research institutional email *and* have prior authorship on a paper already accepted
in that endorsement domain, or obtain personal endorsement from an established arXiv author in
the same domain. arXiv staff cannot waive the requirement. The stated reason is "the flood of
low-quality, non-scientific submissions," and arXiv states it no longer considers institutional
email a sufficient sign of minimum research competence. No submission or rejection figures
accompany the announcement.

The published critique of this architecture is Silagadze's, arXiv:2307.11791 [B] (17). He calls
endorsement a serious step backward from arXiv's original democratic character while conceding it
is a necessary one to block fringe material, argues that moderators rather than readers now judge
whether submissions have value, and warns of arXiv becoming a "closed club" favouring what
moderators consider mainstream. His documented cases, however, are historical journal-refereeing
episodes — Einstein and Rosen's 1936 gravitational-wave manuscript, Joseph Weber's unreplicated
detection claims, early reactions to Feynman's diagrams, Arthur Lunn's destroyed 1921 manuscript
— not arXiv moderation decisions. He proposes a MathOverflow-style reputation model with a small
core of moderators acting sparingly and vote-based removal thresholds no single person can
trigger. Separately, arXiv has removed papers in a live dispute: *Science* reported the removal of
"inflammatory" papers during the room-temperature superconductor controversy (15, 17).

No documented case was found in which a methodological or institutional critique of high-energy
physics was blocked from arXiv. This is recorded as a Tier D rejection.

# Replication: Two Detectors, One Toolchain

Junk and Lyons note that particle physicists rarely use the word "replication" — "replica"
suggests an identical copy — preferring "independent confirmation" for success and "ruling out"
or "refutation" for failure [A] (11). They adopt the National Academies definition: independent
tests using similar analyses with different data or techniques. And they state the key caveat
themselves: shared systematic errors can make replications less independent than they appear.

The historical base rate for independence has been better than the present. LEP ran four
detectors (ALEPH, DELPHI, L3, OPAL); SLAC's PEP ring hosted five [A] (11). The top quark was
found by two rival collaborations with comparably sized datasets. At the LHC, ATLAS and CMS are
the two general-purpose detectors; ALICE and LHCb exist but are specialised for heavy-ion and
forward/flavour physics respectively, so they are not substitutes for a general-purpose
confirmation. The Higgs was announced by ATLAS and CMS, which Junk and Lyons present as a
successful independent confirmation — correctly, at the level of two independently built
detectors with independent collaborations.

Failed replication is documented and the record is not flattering. Junk and Lyons report that
effects at z-scores of 3 and 4 have often not held up as more data arrived. The Θ+ pentaquark was
reported by four experiments in 2003 and six more over the following couple of years; later
studies with far higher event counts saw no evidence, and at least one group failed to confirm
its own original result. The authors' conclusion: "the Θ+ does not exist." The LSND and MiniBooNE
νe excesses remain in unresolved tension with MINOS, MINOS+, Daya Bay and Bugey disappearance
measurements. Junk and Lyons also state that reproducibility does not guarantee reliability: "A
flawed result, when reproduced, contains the same flaws."

Their overall verdict, which this file adopts rather than argues against: "Experimental particle
physicists publish reliable results nearly all of the time," and also "not all results in
experimental particle physics meet the most rigorous standards" [A] (11).

# The Accountability Test Case: OPERA

OPERA's 2011 superluminal-neutrino claim is the cleanest available test of whether accountability
in a large collaboration can be located, and it mostly passes [A] (45).

The result was announced 22–23 September 2011. Two error sources were announced in February 2012:
a fibre-optic link from the GPS receiver to the master clock that had not been fully screwed in,
adding delay and shortening apparent flight times by 73 ns; and an oscillator running faster than
its specified 10 MHz. LNGS confirmed the cable fault in March 2012. Spokesperson Antonio
Ereditato and physics coordinator Dario Autiero resigned their leadership positions on 30 March
2012, following a failed no-confidence vote among more than thirty group team leaders. CERN
declared the neutrino speed consistent with light on 8 June 2012; OPERA published corrected
results on 12 July 2012. Elapsed time from announcement to resolution: roughly nine months.

Named individuals held the accountability, the collaboration's internal governance surfaced the
dispute, and the correction was published. This weighs against the strongest form of the
accountability critique and is recorded in Tier D.

# The 750 GeV Episode: The Theory Layer's Behaviour

The diphoton excess at 750 GeV was reported by ATLAS at 3.9σ local and CMS at 3.4σ local, neither
reaching the 5σ discovery threshold [A] (46). Roughly 500 theoretical papers appeared between the
December 2015 results and August 2016. The 2016 dataset, about four times larger, showed no
bump, and a Bayesian analysis found that the combined data counted as evidence *against* the
state.

Matthew Buckley, a theorist writing in *Boston Review*, counts more than 350 arXiv papers with
"diphoton" or "750" in the title and estimates the true figure above 500 [B] (47). His own
reckoning puts the combined excess at about 2σ after correcting for the look-elsewhere effect —
about one in twenty by chance. His explanation of the behaviour is an explicit incentive
argument: theorists need only paper and pencil, and they answer mainly to peers who control
hiring and tenure, whereas experimentalists are constrained because detector time is shared. He
concedes the field did not need all of the hundreds of papers, defends the enterprise as having
opened some useful theory, and explicitly pushes back on experimental colleagues who argue
theorists should have waited.

The episode is evidence about the *theory* publication layer, not the collaboration layer. The
experimental side behaved correctly: it published the excess at sub-discovery significance with
its significance stated, then published the null. The 500 papers were a response to a published
exclusion-grade result, which is the system working at the experimental layer and overheating at
the theory layer.

# Strategy Governance: The European Process

The CERN Council mandate for the European Strategy for Particle Physics specifies the bodies [A]
(18). The European Strategy Group comprises delegates from the CERN Member States, the
Director-General of CERN, and the Directors of the major European laboratories, appointed by
Council at its European Strategy Session and chaired by the Scientific Secretary. The mandate
does not state how the ESG reaches decisions — consensus or vote — and contains **no
conflict-of-interest provisions**.

The Physics Preparatory Group prepares the scientific contribution — the Briefing Book. Council
appoints its members on the recommendation of the SPC and ECFA; the Strategy Secretary chairs; the
other seats are held by the SPC, ECFA and LDG chairs, a CERN representative, and two Asian and
two American representatives nominated by ICFA's regional representatives. Members were appointed
in September 2024 [A] (19). The eighteen named members:

Karl Jakobs (DE, chair, Scientific Secretary); Philip Burrows (UK, SPC chair, JAI Oxford); Hugh
Montgomery (USA, SPC chair to 31.12.2025); Paraskevas Sphicas (GR, ECFA chair); Mike Seidel (CH,
LDG chair); Pilar Hernandez (ES, IFIC Valencia); Gino Isidori (CH, ETH Zurich); Fabio Maltoni
(BE/IT, Louvain/Bologna); Jocelyn Monroe (UK, Oxford); Tommaso Boccali (IT, INFN Pisa); Thomas
Bergauer (AT, HEPHY Vienna); Cristinel Diaconu (FR, CPPM Marseille); Monica Dunford (DE,
Heidelberg); Gianluigi Arduini (CERN); Anadi Canepa (USA); Xinchou Lou (China); Rogerio Rosenfeld
(Brazil); Yuji Yamazaki (Japan).

All eighteen are high-energy physics researchers or accelerator physicists. That is not an
accident of selection; it is what the appointment rule produces, since nominations come from the
SPC, ECFA and ICFA's regional representatives — bodies whose constituency is the field. The chair
is the former ATLAS Spokesperson (2017–2021), now at Freiburg [A] (51).

The honest counter-finding matters here. The composition is not uniformly collider-interested.
Jocelyn Monroe is an experimental dark-matter direct-detection physicist, deputy spokesperson of
DarkSide-20k and lead of the QUEST-DMC dark-matter search, with a background in MiniBooNE and SNO
[A] (50) — a subfield that competes with a flagship collider for the same European funding
envelope. Pilar Hernandez is a neutrino theorist. The claim that these panels contain *no*
members without a prospective stake in the facility under evaluation is not supported.

Process and outcome [A] (20, 21, 34). CERN solicited community input and received 263–266
submissions from researchers in more than 40 countries, including flagship projects, other
physics areas, national communities, national labs and early-career researchers. Submissions
arrived 31 March, 26 May and 14 November 2025; an open symposium was held in Venice in June
2025; the physics Briefing Book was published 30 September 2025. Seven collider options were
comparatively assessed — FCC-ee, descoped FCC-ee, LCF (including a 250 GeV variant), CLIC, LEP3,
LHeC and a hadron option. Working Group 2b summed precision and BSM reach into a single figure of
merit, scoring gains relative to HL-LHC on a log scale from 1 to 5; costs were taken *from
proponents and experiments*, counting the CERN share. Sixty-two delegates drafted the
recommendations at Ascona, Switzerland, 1–5 December 2025. The ESG recommended FCC-ee as the
preferred option, with a descoped FCC-ee as the preferred alternative and the other options
unranked because of the physics-reach gap. CERN Council deliberates in Budapest in May or June
2026. The ESG advises; it does not bind Council.

Two details in that process deserve flagging. Costs were sourced from proponents. And descoping
options — removing the top-quark run (saving 1.26 BCHF), going from four interaction regions to
two (0.80 BCHF), reducing RF power from 50 MW to 30 MW (0.35 BCHF), with luminosity dropping to
0.36 of the full programme — appeared as a new development *at* the Ascona drafting meeting (21),
not in the earlier community consultation. No dissenting view is recorded in the CERN Courier
account of Ascona (20).

The FCC Feasibility Study does have an independence provision, and it should be credited. The FCC
Scientific Advisory Committee comprises up to 16 international experts who are "not directly
involved in the Feasibility Study," appointed by the Steering Committee, meeting about twice a
year, covering accelerators, technical infrastructure, key technologies, physics and detectors;
the page proposes the Chair become an ex-officio SPC member to give Council "an independent
scientific and technical assessment" [A] (26). The committee roster is not published on that
page.

# Strategy Governance: The US Process and Its Conflict-of-Interest Regime

The US process has an explicit conflict-of-interest provision, which makes it the better test
case for C5 — and the content of that provision is the finding.

P5 is a temporary subcommittee of HEPAP, which advises the DOE Office of Science High Energy
Physics program and the NSF Division of Physics [A] (25). DOE and NSF issued the charge to the
HEPAP chair on 2 November 2022. The panel was assembled by the end of 2022 in consultation with
DOE and NSF; JoAnne Hewett, Karsten Heeger and Hitoshi Murayama led the selection, balancing
scientific expertise, geography, DOE versus NSF representation, age and international membership
[A] (24). It drew on the Snowmass 2021 community study: over 1,500 two-page Letters of Interest,
over 500 white papers, ten Frontiers, about 80 Topical Groups, about 80 cross-Frontier liaisons,
and roughly 1,350 participants at the Seattle Community Summer Study in July 2022 (~700 in
person, ~650 remote) [A] (52).

The 32-member P5 roster is published with institutions [A] (22, 23): Murayama (chair, UC
Berkeley); Heeger (deputy chair, Yale); Asai (Tokyo); Ballarino (CERN); Bose (Wisconsin-Madison);
Cranmer (Wisconsin-Madison); Cyr-Racine (New Mexico); Demers (Yale); Geddes (LBNL); Gershtein
(Rutgers); Heinemann (DESY); Hewett (HEPAP chair ex officio to May 2023, SLAC); Huber (Virginia
Tech); Mahn (Michigan State); Mandelbaum (Carnegie Mellon); Maricic (Hawaii at Manoa); Merkel
(Fermilab); Monahan (William & Mary); Onyisi (Texas at Austin); Palmer (Brookhaven); Raubenheimer
(SLAC/Stanford); Sanchez (Florida State); Schnee (South Dakota Mines); Seidel (interim HEPAP
chair ex officio from June 2023, New Mexico); Seo (IBS/Fermilab); Thaler (MIT); Touramanis
(Liverpool); Vieregg (Chicago); Weinstein (Iowa State); Winslow (MIT); Yu (Oregon); Zwaska
(Fermilab).

Every member is a particle or accelerator physicist. There were no project-management
specialists, which made independent verification of cost and schedule information difficult (25).
The panel's cost subcommittee was charged with obtaining and clarifying cost, schedule and risk
information from *proponents* of projects over $250M and assessing it "at a high level," while
explicitly *not* preparing its own estimates [A] (23).

Appendix A.7 of the P5 report is titled "Conflict of Interest Resolution." Its content is narrow
and specific [A] (22). It concerns the HEPAP vote of 8 December 2023 on acceptance of the P5
Report, and records that four members "have been determined to have conflicts of interest due to
their employment at US national laboratories" and will not participate in that vote. The cost
subcommittee's process section adds one line: "Possible conflicts of interest taken account in
assignment to subgroups."

So a conflict-of-interest regime exists. Its operative boundary is *employer* — employment at a
US national laboratory — applied to a single acceptance vote by the parent committee. It is not
drawn at the level of scientific programme: a university-based member whose research group's
funding depends on a recommended facility is not within its scope. P5's recommendations included
targeted R&D toward a 10 TeV muon collider, with a demonstrator facility that "could be built at
Fermilab" — the "Muon Shot" — while conceding it is not yet known whether a muon collider is
feasible [A] (22).

The second falsification condition for C5 asks whether recommendations have historically gone
against the interest of the membership. There is at least partial evidence that they have: the
2014 P5 named the HL-LHC, the ILC and LBNF among its three high-priority large projects, and by
2022 the ILC had been shelved and several projects were delayed or facing cost gaps [A] (25).
P5's 2023 report recommended CMB-S4, which NSF and DOE then cancelled (25). A panel whose
recommendations are overridden or whose projects die is not a panel with reliable capture of the
funding decision. The 2023 charge also forced the panel to work within hostile budget scenarios:
a 2% annual increase that physicists called "grim," versus full CHIPS and Science Act funding
plus 3%, with rising operating and energy costs to be absorbed (25).

# The SSC: Cost Escalation and the Mechanics of Cancellation

All figures nominal, as published [A] (27, 28).

Cost trajectory. Congress was told in 1987 that the project could be completed for $4.4 billion.
In early 1993 the General Accounting Office reported a $630 million overrun against a $1.25
billion construction budget. In March 1993 the *New York Times* reported the estimated total had
grown to $8.4 billion. Estimates rising "to $12bn" are cited as a cancellation factor. A June
1993 Inspector General draft audit found inadequate documentation for $203 million — 40% of
spending to that point — and investigated about $500,000 in questionable expenses.

Spending and termination. About $2 billion was spent: roughly $1.6 billion in federal
construction funds across FY1988–93, plus $279 million from Texas together with land and certain
services (one account puts the Texas total nearer $400 million). At cancellation, 22.5 km of
tunnel and 17 shafts were complete. Congress made $735 million available for termination ($640M
appropriated for FY1994 plus $95M carried over). GAO's termination estimate fell from about $1.1
billion over five years (October 1993) to $695 million (February 1994) to $568 million (mid-March
1994) — the last being $167 million *less* than available funds, which is why GAO concluded that
additional termination funds were not justified. Texas claimed a $539 million refund, not
included in that estimate.

Political mechanics. In 1992 the House voted against the project 231–181, but it survived
reconciliation on Senate support of 62–32. In early 1993 a contractor-funded group lobbied
Congress. In June 1993 President Clinton wrote to Congress asking for continued support, arguing
that abandonment would compromise US leadership in basic science. On 19 October 1993 the House
rejected further funding and Senate negotiators failed to restore it; Rep. Jim Slattery was
credited with orchestrating the House action. Clinton signed the cancelling bill on 30 October
1993.

Reasons on the record: rising costs; poor management by physicists and DOE officials; the end of
the Cold War removing the prestige rationale; a belief that smaller experiments of comparable
merit could be funded for the same money; federal budget pressure against a $255 billion deficit;
Texas political reluctance, with Governor Ann Richards unwilling to fight for it; and scientific
opposition, notably from condensed-matter physicists arguing the project was unreasonably
expensive while other fields were underfunded. International funding failed: Europe stayed with
CERN, India pledged $50 million, and talks with Japan collapsed amid trade tensions — promoting
the project as a demonstration of American superiority is itself cited as a hindrance. Cost
comparisons to NASA's ISS contribution recurred on both sides.

Which lessons were claimed versus applied. The FCC process does show two structural answers to
the SSC's failure modes: an external Scientific Advisory Committee of experts not involved in the
study (26), and a proposed "phase-gate" process with reviews every five years, with the study
team advising against an early decision on FCC-hh given the magnet technology-readiness gap (33).
Those are real institutional responses to "escalating costs and management problems." What was
not applied is independent cost estimation: in both the European and US processes, cost figures
come from the proponents (21, 23). The SSC's own escalation was discovered by GAO and the
Inspector General — external auditors — not by the physics community.

The currencies differ and no deflator is applied here, so the ratio is not computed. What the
table shows unambiguously is direction: the headline price rose while the tunnel shortened, the

the cost estimate as not yet fully reliable, with reliability expected to improve after further
accelerator-system development and subsurface investigations from 2024 (33).

Funding structure [A] (31, 32, 34). CERN could cover about 65% of stage-one construction from its
existing budget, leaving a shortfall of more than CHF 5 billion in additional contributions.
Funding scenarios were expected in the Feasibility Study but deferred. The Feasibility Study
itself was budgeted at CHF 100 million and cost CHF 113 million (~US$128 million). CERN's
operating budget is about CHF 1.4 billion per year. In December 2025 private philanthropists
pledged €860 million (~US$1 billion) toward the member-state approval bid (54). The CERN Council
decision is expected around 2028.

Named reservations on the record [A] [B]. John Womersley, special advisor at Edinburgh and former
UK delegate to the CERN Council, warned that member states could be "locked into something like a
70-year mortgage" and said "Almost no one that I have spoken to thinks this is a good idea" (32).
Patrick Koppenburg, chair of the Advisory Committee of CERN Users, framed the FCC as a bet on
what physics the community will want in about 2070 (34). Vladimir Shiltsev of Northern Illinois
University found the cost estimates broadly consistent with his own 2023 study, with a few
technical questions remaining (32).

# The FCC Debate, Cited From Both Sides

**Hossenfelder**, from her own writing [B] (35). Her argument is that the LHC found the Higgs and
no other new particles, which shows the arguments for them were mistaken, and that there is "no
reason to think" a larger collider would do more than refine Standard Model measurements. She
says particle physicists have "failed to come up with any reason why going to higher energies is
currently a promising route forward." She puts the price at roughly $20 billion and argues the
money would do more in astrophysical dark-matter searches, gravitational-wave detectors,
low-energy precision measurements, fusion, climate science or data infrastructure: "$20 billion
that go into a particle collider are $20 billion that do not go into nuclear fusion." She argues
collider costs may fall with wakefield acceleration, so waiting could make sense. She
characterises naturalness and the WIMP miracle as metaphysical, belief-based motivations, and the
field's reliance on beauty as a predictive criterion as groupthink. On conduct: advocates who
ignore past failures and refuse comparison with alternative investments "do not want to have a
sincere discussion" and are "merely lobbying." She has separately criticised a CERN promotional
video for listing open problems a collider would at best partly address (54).

**Arkani-Hamed**, from his own words in a March 2019 *CERN Courier* interview [B] (36). He does
not use "naturalness"; he describes the Higgs mass as a deep puzzle, noting that "simple estimates
for the Higgs mass from its interactions with virtual particles in the vacuum are wildly wrong"
and that "Nature clearly has other ideas for what the Higgs is about than theorists do." He reads
the LHC's null supersymmetry results as showing that explaining the Higgs mass "must involve an
even larger paradigm change" than expected — that is, he treats the null result as informative
rather than as a reason to stop. He argues the Higgs "is the most important actor in this story
amenable to experimental study," that a Higgs factory could test substructure and a 100 TeV
machine could measure the self-coupling to a few per cent, and calls disappointment at finding
only the Higgs "completely backwards." He concedes "there is absolutely no guarantee we will
produce new particles" and frames the case as exploration: "we go to high energies because it's
the frontier, and we look around for new things." Three direct responses to critics: non-experts
do not make the strongest case against colliders; waiting on accelerator breakthroughs is naïve
because "we can't plan doing science around miracles"; and science funding is not zero-sum, so
collider money would not otherwise fund other experiments. He names C. N. Yang's critique as a
serious attack and points to David Gross's reply.

The zero-sum disagreement is the crux, and it is an empirical question neither side has settled
here. Hossenfelder's opportunity-cost argument requires fungibility; Arkani-Hamed's rebuttal
requires that large-facility funding is additive. The DOE HEP figures below bear on it: the FY2026
request cut total HEP by $111.7 million year-on-year, with Energy Frontier Experimental Physics
falling from $152.3M to $111.9M and Cosmic Frontier from $108.4M to $79.1M — movements within a
constrained envelope, not an expanding one.

**Prescod-Weinstein and Tait**, in *Slate*, 31 January 2019, responding to Hossenfelder's *New
York Times* op-ed [B] (37). Their central point is that a null result is informative: "When an
experimental result indicates that one particular idea is not correct, this is neither a failure
of the experiment nor of the original idea itself." They argue the critique misreads science as
being about the glory of discovery, when finding no new particles reveals "the distance between
human imagination and the real world"; that the LHC had a clear purpose because the Standard
Model required the Higgs or something like it, and the Higgs's interactions match prediction to
roughly 10 per cent; and that most theoretical ideas are expected to be wrong. They accept the
need for prioritisation, point to the European and Snowmass strategy reviews as the proper forum,
and argue a collider's cost is modest relative to other government spending when spread over
decades and shared among nations.

**C. N. Yang and David Gross** on the Chinese collider, 2016 [B]. Yang, a Nobel laureate and
field insider, publicly opposed China building a large collider; Gross replied in defence. The
primary sources for this exchange could not be retrieved within this session's search budget, so
it is recorded here as a documented exchange with content unverified from the originals — flagged
for a later pass.

# Career Incentives and What Happens to Critics

The career-structure figures are documented. The "critics get punished" claim largely is not, and
the distinction is the point of this section.

**Structure** [A] (42, 43, 44). HEP postdoc appointments typically run five to six years, which
the Snowmass career-pipeline report describes as longer than in other disciplines; among
tenure-track faculty surveyed, the average postdoc period was 5.1 years and the average faculty
job search 21.2 months. The report states that the faculty job market has "stayed fairly
plateaued over the years" while the number of postdocs and PhDs has not kept pace, but gives no
applicant-per-position ratio — a gap worth noting, since the oversupply claim is usually asserted
numerically. Almost 50% of surveyed HEP alumni left after a student or postdoc position, before
reaching a faculty or scientist role; about two-thirds of physics degree holders work in
industry. The 2021 community survey (1,014 respondents reaching the career section; 92.3% in
academia; 38.4% early-career, 31.3% faculty, 18% scientists) found 86% of postdocs and 77% of PhD
students applying or planning to apply for jobs; of early-career scientists who accepted offers,
nearly 73% took postdocs and about 16% tenure-track positions; 45.4% of PhD students and 39.6% of
postdocs said their salary probably or definitely was not adequate; 56.6% of faculty and 62.3% of
scientists had considered leaving. Broader physics figures: historically 10–20% of physics PhD
students obtain permanent academic jobs, against a Royal Society finding that under 0.5% of
science PhD students become full professors and 3.5% obtain lower-ranking permanent university
research posts.

**Authorship as currency.** The mechanism is structural rather than anecdotal. Authorship is a
membership right requiring 80 days of qualifying service (6), author order is alphabetical and
uninformative (4), no per-author contribution statements are published (3, 8), and some
collaborations charge institutional entry and annual authorship fees (42). The Snowmass report's
own recommendation — that entry fees scale with the number of PhD holders, with alternatives such
as "single PI" membership or facility access without full authorship — concedes that authorship
is being purchased and bundled. The *h*-index favours experimentalists in large collaborations
over theorists publishing in small groups (4). Klein's complaint is the hiring-side version: a
name on a paper may not be meaningful for tenure decisions, where colleagues have to ask what the
person actually did (5).

**What happens to critics.** The brief flagged this as the place to be careful, and that caution
was warranted. The evidence does not support a pattern.

Alessandro Strumia is the case most often cited, and it does not fit [A] (48, 55). CERN removed
his slides and suspended his "invited scientist" position on 30 September–1 October 2018, citing
a Code of Conduct breach for naming a CERN employee in a talk on gender at CERN's first Workshop
on High Energy Theory and Gender; the University of Pisa opened an ethical investigation on 1
October 2018 and announced a sanction on 18 January 2019; CERN confirmed on 7 March 2019 that his
guest-professor status would not continue. A public letter condemning the talk gathered nearly
4,000 signatures. None of this concerns a critique of collider funding or HEP methodology.
Strumia subsequently published a guest post opposing a giant new collider on Hossenfelder's blog
and published gender bibliometrics in *Quantitative Science Studies* in 2021, which drew four
critical commentaries and his reply — adversarial engagement, not suppression.

Sabine Hossenfelder's case is partly her own account and should be reported as such [B] (49). By
April 2024 she had left full-time academic employment for full-time YouTube work; she states
publicly that she did not "quit academia" or "leave academia" and that this characterisation was
spread by others via Wikipedia. Her LMU/MCMP affiliation ended in 2025; the only source for this
is her own September 2025 video, and the Wikipedia entry flags it as needing a non-primary
source. Her critiques were published in the *New York Times*, on her own widely read blog, in a
trade book, and on arXiv — none of which was blocked. Independent documentation of a causal link
between the critique and the end of the affiliation was not found.

Against the pattern claim: C. N. Yang publicly opposed a Chinese collider as a Nobel laureate
insider; John Womersley, a former UK delegate to the CERN Council, published a sharply negative
assessment of the FCC's funding structure (32); Matthew Buckley published self-critical
reflection on his own community's behaviour (47); Prescod-Weinstein and Tait argued the opposite
side in *Slate* (37); the Snowmass career reports and the Reinterpretation Forum report are
critical documents produced *inside* the field and published under its own imprint. No systematic
evidence of professional penalty for publishing a critique of HEP was found. This is recorded in
Tier D.

# Structural Inferences

Each of these is Tier C. None is a finding. Each states the Tier A facts it rests on and what
would falsify it.

**C-I. The audit function sits entirely inside the collaboration that produced the result.** [C]
Rests on: the Reinterpretation Forum's inventory of what is not published — full likelihoods
(two ATLAS analyses at the time of writing), search-region correlations, per-process background
decompositions, digitised detector response, executable analysis code and frameworks, generator
inputs, and raw data for refits that is "often not provided" (12); the statement that
machine-learning analyses "cannot be reproduced at all outside the experimental collaboration"
(12); Junk and Lyons on missing correlated-uncertainty information and on collaboration approval
as a barrier to external reproduction (11); and the elaborate internal review chain that does
have access to all of it (9, 10, 11). It follows that a journal referee, working from the
submitted manuscript, can audit presentation, statistical treatment as described, and internal
consistency, but cannot independently verify the acceptance corrections, background estimates or
selection efficiencies on which the result rests. The internal review is therefore not a
supplement to external audit; it is a substitute for it. *Falsified by*: documentation that
referees of ATLAS or CMS papers are given internal analysis notes, likelihoods or code under
embargo; or a documented case where a journal referee forced a substantive change to an LHC
physics result. Neither was found, but absence of evidence here is weak — referee reports are
confidential, and this is a searchable question for a later pass.

**C-II. HEP's exemplary null-publication record and its weak external auditability have the same
cause.** [C] Rests on: the collaboration being the publishing unit, with authorship a membership
right and the collaboration-wide review the final gate (6, 7, 9, 10); and the resulting pattern
of thousands of exclusion-limit papers with essentially one discovery (2, 14) alongside the
reinterpretation deficit (12). Because the collaboration publishes everything it analyses, and
because a null exclusion limit is its normal product rather than a career setback for any named
individual, there is no file drawer. The same structure means no outsider holds the inputs. The
field's greatest publication virtue and its auditability weakness are two consequences of one
institutional fact. This is the inference in this file with the most explanatory reach and the
least prior assembly. *Falsified by*: evidence of a HEP file drawer — analyses completed inside
ATLAS or CMS and never published because the result was null; or demonstration that external
reproducibility is in fact high.

**C-III. The conflict-of-interest boundary is drawn at employer, not at scientific programme.**
[C] Rests on: P5 Appendix A.7, which recuses four members for "employment at US national
laboratories" from a single HEPAP acceptance vote (22); the ESPP mandate, which contains no
conflict-of-interest provisions at all (18); the FCC Scientific Advisory Committee's
non-involvement requirement, which excludes study participation but not the field (26); and the
fact that cost figures in both processes come from proponents (21, 23). The regime is formally
present and orthogonal to the interest actually at stake, which is not employment by a lab but
dependence of one's research programme on the facility under evaluation. *Falsified by*: an
unpublished recusal register covering programme-level interests; or evidence that
programme-interested members abstained from the recommendations themselves rather than only from
a parent-committee vote.

**C-IV. The composition rule, not the individuals, is what forecloses independence.** [C] Rests
on: the appointment mechanism — PPG members appointed by Council on SPC and ECFA recommendation
with ICFA regional nominations (19); P5 members selected by three senior HEP figures in
consultation with DOE and NSF (24); and the resulting rosters of eighteen and thirty-two members
in which every seat is held by a HEP or accelerator physicist (19, 22). The set from which panels
are drawn is defined by the field, so no member is positioned to ask whether the field should
receive the money at all. This is a weaker claim than C5 as registered: it does not assert that
members are self-interested or that the assessment is dishonest. It asserts that the question
"should this discipline get this envelope" is structurally outside the panels' composition.
*Falsified by*: voting members from outside HEP — economists, science-policy specialists,
project-management professionals, or researchers from competing disciplines. Note that the P5
report's own acknowledged absence of project-management specialists (25) is an instance of the
same rule operating, and it was recorded by the panel's observers as a practical handicap in
verifying cost and schedule.

**C-V. Authorship as membership right relocates accountability to an entity that cannot be
sanctioned.** [C] Rests on: the 80-day qualification rule producing authorship on every paper
(6); the absence of responsibility for content from the Authorship Committee's remit (6); the
absence of per-author contribution statements (3, 8); Klein's statement that authors need not
have read the paper (5); and the alphabetical convention carrying no contribution information
(4). The responsible unit therefore becomes the collaboration, which has no legal personality,
cannot be defunded as a unit without defunding the facility, and persists across complete
turnover of membership. *Falsified by*: a documented case in which a HEP collaboration was
sanctioned as an entity, or in which individual authors bore professional consequence for a
collaboration paper's error. OPERA is the partial counter-example and it cuts the other way (see
Tier D): named leaders resigned.

**C-VI. Cost estimates sourced from proponents, in both the European and US processes, reproduce
the information structure that produced the SSC's escalation.** [C] Rests on: the ESPP working
group taking costs "from proponents and experiments" (21); the P5 cost subcommittee charged to
obtain information from proponents and explicitly *not* to prepare its own estimates (23); the
FCC cost trajectory from ~€9bn to CHF 15.3bn with a shorter tunnel, lower hadron-stage energy and
a fifteen-year slip (29, 30, 31, 32); CERN Courier's own description of the estimate as not yet
fully reliable (33); and the SSC record in which escalation from $4.4bn to $8.4–12bn and
inadequate documentation for 40% of spending were established by GAO and the Inspector General,
external auditors, not by the community (27, 28). *Falsified by*: an independent cost audit of
the FCC by a body outside the physics community, with published methodology. The phase-gate
review proposal (33) and the FCC Scientific Advisory Committee (26) are partial answers but are
internal to the field, and the SAC roster is not published.

# Claims Examined and Not Supported

Tier D. These were examined in this stream and could not be supported, or had to be substantially
narrowed. Recorded so they are not re-litigated.

**D1. HEP suppresses or under-publishes null results.** Rejected, and the opposite is closer to
true. Fanelli's 20-discipline study places space science lowest in frequency of positive results
and physics joint 12th–13th of 20 by odds ratio, well below materials science, psychology,
immunology and economics (13). ATLAS and CMS have published over 2,000 papers (2), overwhelmingly
searches and measurements consistent with the Standard Model, with exclusion contours as the
standard published deliverable and summary plots aggregating them (14). A null search is the
expected product, not a career setback for any named author. This is a genuine strength of the
field and any critique that implies otherwise is wrong on the evidence. The separable and
surviving point is that publishing a limit is not publishing the means to re-derive it (12).

**D2. There is no conflict-of-interest regime in HEP strategy governance.** Rejected as stated.
P5's report carries an explicit appendix, A.7 "Conflict of Interest Resolution," and four members
were recused from the HEPAP acceptance vote for national-laboratory employment (22). The cost
subcommittee states that possible conflicts were taken into account in subgroup assignment (23).
The FCC Scientific Advisory Committee is constituted from experts not directly involved in the
Feasibility Study (26). The surviving claim is narrower and is carried as C-III: the regime's
boundary is employer, not scientific programme. The European process is weaker on this point —
the ESPP mandate contains no conflict-of-interest provisions (18) — but absence from a mandate
page is not proof that no practice exists, and this should not be overstated.

**D3. Strategy panels contain no members without a prospective stake in the facility under
evaluation.** Rejected. This was one of the frame document's two explicit falsification
conditions for the governance critique, and it fails. Jocelyn Monroe sits on the Physics
Preparatory Group and is an experimental dark-matter direct-detection physicist, deputy
spokesperson of DarkSide-20k and lead of the QUEST-DMC dark-matter search (19, 50) — a subfield
competing with a flagship collider for the same funding envelope. Pilar Hernandez is a neutrino
theorist. On P5, Rachel Mandelbaum and Francis-Yan Cyr-Racine come from cosmology rather than
collider physics (22). The composition is drawn from one discipline, which is C-IV, but it is not
uniformly composed of collider beneficiaries.

**D4. Strategy panels reliably capture the funding decision in their own interest.** Not
supported. The 2014 P5 named the ILC among three high-priority large projects; by 2022 the ILC
had been shelved and several projects were delayed or facing cost gaps (25). The 2023 P5 report
recommended CMB-S4, which NSF and DOE then cancelled (25). The ESG advises CERN Council and does
not bind it (20). The FY2026 DOE request cuts total HEP by $111.7 million against FY2025 enacted,
with Energy Frontier Experimental Physics down from $152.3M to $111.9M (39). Panels whose
flagship recommendations are shelved or cancelled, operating against declining appropriations,
are not panels with reliable capture.

**D5. Collaboration-internal review is a rubber stamp.** Rejected. The CMS 1,000th-paper record
shows a result rebuilt over nine months after internal criticism of insufficient sensitivity, a
new analysis tool required to pass separate quality control, multiple rounds with conveners and
an external Analysis Review Committee before unblinding was permitted, and a revision at
collaboration-wide review to correct a misleading interpretation that collaboration readers had
flagged (9). ATLAS Editorial Boards decide whether an analysis is worth publishing at all (10).
Statistics committees of at least six members advise on systematics, and dissenting collaborators
can have their names removed from the author list (11). Blind analysis is standard practice
specifically to prevent outcome-directed tuning (11).

**D6. External peer review of HEP papers is worthless.** Too strong, and not the claim worth
making. Journal review audits presentation, statistical treatment as described, internal
consistency and interpretation — the same functions it performs in every field. No field's
two-referee review re-derives a result from raw data. The defensible claim is the comparative one
in C-I: in HEP the ratio of what internal review can check to what external review can check is
far more lopsided than in a field where a referee could in principle obtain the data, and the
field's own 2020 report documents why (12).

**D7. Hyperauthorship has concealed error or misconduct in HEP.** Not supported. No documented HEP
retraction or misconduct case was found in which diffusion of responsibility across a large
author list prevented the error from being located. OPERA is the available test case and it points
the other way: the fault was identified (a loose fibre-optic connection and an out-of-spec
oscillator), named leaders — Spokesperson Antonio Ereditato and physics coordinator Dario Autiero
— resigned their leadership positions on 30 March 2012 after a failed no-confidence vote among
more than thirty group team leaders, and the corrected result was published within roughly nine
months of the original announcement (45). Hyperauthorship creates an accountability *structure*
problem (C-V); it has not been shown to have produced an accountability *failure*.

**D8. arXiv moderation suppresses critiques of high-energy physics.** Not supported. No case was
found of a methodological or institutional critique of HEP being blocked from arXiv.
Hossenfelder, Silagadze and Strumia all published their critiques, on arXiv or in high-visibility
venues. Silagadze's own documented cases of contested gatekeeping are historical journal-
refereeing episodes (Einstein–Rosen 1936, Weber, Feynman, Lunn), not arXiv decisions (17). The
real and documented concerns are procedural and should be stated as such: appeal decisions are
final with no feedback given, moderation is confidential, "lack of originality, novelty, or
significance" is a broad discretionary ground, no rejection statistics are published (15), and the
January 2026 endorsement tightening narrows first-time access to those with prior accepted
authorship in the domain or a personal endorser (16). Those are structural gatekeeping facts. They
are not evidence of suppression of HEP critique.

**D9. Critics of HEP are professionally punished.** Not supported on the available evidence, and
this was the claim most in need of discipline. The Strumia case, most often invoked, concerns a
Code of Conduct breach in a talk on gender at CERN — he named a CERN employee — not a critique of
collider funding or methodology; he published a collider-sceptical guest post afterwards and
published gender bibliometrics in a peer-reviewed journal in 2021 (48, 55). Hossenfelder's
departure from full-time academic employment is her own account, she explicitly denies having
"quit academia," and the end of her LMU affiliation is sourced only to her own video with the
Wikipedia entry flagging the need for independent sourcing (49). Her critiques were published in
the *New York Times*, in a trade book, on arXiv and on her blog without obstruction. On the other
side: C. N. Yang opposed a Chinese collider as a Nobel-laureate insider; John Womersley, a former
UK delegate to CERN Council, published a sharply negative FCC assessment (32); Matthew Buckley
published self-critical reflection on his own community (47); and the field's most damaging
internal documents — the Reinterpretation Forum report (12) and the Snowmass career reports
(42, 43) — were produced and published inside the field. An anecdote about one person's contract
is not a pattern, and this file does not assert one.

**D10. The SSC's lessons were claimed but not applied.** Partially rejected. Two structural
answers to the SSC's documented failure modes are in place: an external Scientific Advisory
Committee of experts not directly involved in the Feasibility Study (26), and a phase-gate review
process with five-yearly reviews and explicit advice against an early FCC-hh decision given the
magnet technology-readiness gap (33). The surviving criticism is specific and is carried as C-VI:
cost estimates still come from proponents in both the European and US processes (21, 23), and the
SSC's escalation was established by GAO and the Inspector General rather than by the community
(27, 28).

**D11. Conflict of interest in expert panels is a HEP-specific problem.** Rejected. Expert
advisory panels across every discipline are composed of practitioners, for the straightforward
reason that assessing technical feasibility requires technical expertise. Expertise and interest
genuinely cannot be fully separated: a panel of non-specialists could not evaluate a 14 T magnet
programme or a figure of merit for electroweak precision. What is specific to HEP is scale and
concentration — a single facility decision commits CHF 15.3 billion and roughly fifteen years of
one discipline's entire experimental programme, and the same community both proposes and assesses
it. That is a difference of degree, not of kind, and it should not be presented as a difference of
kind.

**D12. Postdoc oversupply in HEP is quantified.** Not supported in the sources located. The
Snowmass career-pipeline report states only that the faculty market has "stayed fairly plateaued"
while postdoc and PhD numbers have not kept pace, and it gives no applicant-per-position ratio
(42). The available hard numbers are adjacent rather than direct: HEP postdocs lasting five to
six years, an average 5.1-year postdoc and 21.2-month search among tenure-track faculty surveyed,
almost 50% of alumni leaving after a student or postdoc position (42, 43), and a general physics
figure of 10–20% of PhD students obtaining permanent academic jobs (44). The oversupply claim is
usually asserted with a ratio. No such ratio for HEP was found, and this file does not supply one.
