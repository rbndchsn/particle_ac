Cost, Procurement and the Money Trail

A collection-phase financial record of high-energy physics — audits, overruns, single-supplier procurement, compensation, prosecutions, critic claims and unverified allegations, each with provenance

v12026-11-2573 referencescollection phase — not an assessment; every item carries a source class and a status tag, and nothing encountered has been discarded

What the collection holds

  • The $203m, chased to source — and it is not an audit figure. It traces to a 1993 New York Times report of a DOE Inspector General draft. The verified figure is DOE/IG-0389 (1996): “The OIG questioned $207 million in contractor costs.” No document states the two are the same, and the implied denominator (~$508m) is inconsistent with total SSC spend, so the audit covered a sub-period or a single contractor.
  • On the same project, on the same date, DOE's own estimate showed a $124m overrun while an independent Departmental assessment showed ~$1.6bn — a factor of thirteen.
  • DOE-OIG-23-29: Fermilab's operator had $162,018,139.47 questioned of $413.9m claimed for FY2018, of which $159.47m was unsupported indirect costs — and DOE “did not review or approve” the contractor's indirect cost rates.
  • FCC-ee: 10.5 → 15.32 BCHF (+45.9%) while the tunnel shrank 100 → 90.7 km. FCC-hh rose while design energy fell 100 → 85 TeV and magnet field 16 → 14 T. CERN's own 2023 model priced a 25%-overrun risk case at 13.8 BCHF; the actual estimate exceeded it.
  • HL-LHC baseline 950 → 1,140 MCHF (+20%) in a document stating changes were “limited within +10%”, with no risk contingency in the baseline at all, by policy.
  • Three named LHC suppliers carry criminal or competition findings in other markets: Alstom pleaded guilty to FCPA books-and-records falsification ($772.29m); Furukawa Electric to Sherman Act bid rigging and price fixing ($200m, three executives jailed); Air Liquide and Linde — the world's only two qualified helium turbo-expander makers — were both named in a Brazilian market-allocation case.
  • The structural finding. CERN's own papers name the “risk and high cost of sole supplier” for Nb₃Sn, a 40% weight-price premium, degrading yield at the single qualified vendor, and $13/kA·m against $7.50 in 2005. Nobody has ever benchmarked or audited that pricing. CERN publishes its non-competitive-award threshold but not the count or value; publishes no internal audit output, no senior remuneration, no interest register, no post-employment restriction; and is immune from national jurisdiction, so a bid protest cannot exist. The conditions for overcharging are documented by CERN itself. The data to test it is structurally unavailable to outsiders.

What cuts the other way

  • Particle physics is not a cost-overrun outlier. JWST +870%, ITER +340%, LBNF/DUNE +286%, SSC +108%, NIF +57–88%, LHC +65%, FCC-ee +46%, SKA +36%, HL-LHC +20%. The two worst are not particle physics.
  • GAO-08-641 found roughly 70% of 42 DOE Office of Science projects within committed cost, and 89% of completed ones — better than the megaproject norm.
  • DOE-OIG-26-39 found zero questioned costs at SLAC across four fiscal years.
  • No prosecution, debarment, bid protest or contract dispute arising from a CERN contract exists anywhere in the record. The supplier misconduct above is all in other markets.
  • The fringe allegations (CERN “collected trillions”, undisclosed Rothschild and Rockefeller donations, audit impossible under Swiss banking law) are recorded in full with the contradicting documents named inline — the United States is not a CERN member state, and audits by Poland's NIK and the Portuguese Court of Auditors are published.

How to read this file#

This is a collection document. It is not an assessment, and the only judgements made in it are about provenance and verification status. Assessment is a separate, later phase.

Every item carries two tags.

Source class

  • [SRC: audit] — official audit or inspector-general report
  • [SRC: court] — court filing, judgment, prosecution, debarment record
  • [SRC: official] — institutional financial statement, budget document, procurement record
  • [SRC: press] — journalism, outlet named
  • [SRC: advocacy] — campaign or interest group, named
  • [SRC: anon] — forum, blog, anonymous or unattributable claim

Status

  • VERIFIED — the figure or finding appears in the primary document named, read directly
  • REPORTED — stated by a named secondary source that cites or summarises a primary document not read here
  • ALLEGED — asserted by an identified party; not established
  • UNVERIFIABLE — no document class exists, or none is public, that could settle it

Recording an allegation is not asserting it. Where a claim is attributed, the attribution is part of the record. Nothing here says a named individual committed a crime; where allegations touch individuals they are stated as attributed allegations, with the source.

1. The Superconducting Super Collider#

1.1 What was spent, and on what#

Total spent at termination. About $2 billion had been spent when Congress voted to terminate in October 1993; from FY1988 to FY1993 the US spent about $1.6 billion on construction, and Texas contributed $279 million plus land and services (1). [SRC: audit] VERIFIED. The DOE Inspector General's own later summary gives the figure as "about $1.57 billion" spent before the 28 October 1993 termination (2). [SRC: audit] VERIFIED. Verification note: the $2bn and $1.57bn figures are not reconciled in either document; the difference is probably construction-only versus all-sources, but neither says so.

The land and the hole. The project left about 15 miles of underground tunnels and access shafts (1), described elsewhere as 22.5 km of tunnel and 17 shafts (5), and "14 miles of tunnels" with $2bn spent, "about 20 percent" of a projected $11 billion project (7). [SRC: audit] / [SRC: official] VERIFIED for the GAO figure; REPORTED for the others. Verification note: the 14-mile / 15-mile / 22.5 km figures differ; the authoritative number would be the final DOE site-restoration completion report.

What happened to the tunnel. GAO reported that technical consultants advised DOE that letting the tunnel fill with water was preferable and cheaper than refilling it, and this became part of DOE's revised lower-cost site restoration plan; GAO did not question it (1). [SRC: audit] VERIFIED.

What happened to the site. Multimillionaire J.B. Hunt bought the site in 2006 for $6.5 million for a Tier IV data centre; Hunt died shortly afterwards and the site fell into disuse; in 2012 Magnablend, a Waxahachie industrial chemical manufacturer, bought it, price not given (7). [SRC: official] REPORTED. Verification note: the GSA or DOE disposal records would give the exact conveyance terms and proceeds.

1.3 The $203 million — chased to source#

This figure is widely repeated. Its chain is as follows.

The circulating claim. "Inadequate documentation for $203 million in project spending, or 40% of the money spent up to that point," attributed to the Inspector General (5). The reference given is Hilts, Philip J., "Energy Chief Says Accounting Problems Snag Supercollider Project," New York Times, 1 July 1993 (5). [SRC: press] REPORTED. Verification note: the NYT article of 1 July 1993 is the proximate source; the underlying document is the DOE IG draft audit then circulating. Neither was retrievable in this pass. What would settle it: the DOE OIG draft audit report of mid-1993 on SSC contractor costs, or the final version of it, obtainable by FOIA to DOE OIG.

A separate and larger figure, verified. The DOE Inspector General's 1996 summary report states plainly: "The OIG questioned $207 million in contractor costs" (2). [SRC: audit] VERIFIED.

Reading of the relationship. The $203m figure is a 1993 press report of inadequate documentation covering 40% of spend to date; the $207m figure is the 1996 OIG summary's own figure for questioned contractor costs. They are close enough that they are plausibly the same audit line at two stages of its life, but no document read here states that they are the same number, and "inadequately documented" and "questioned" are not the same audit category. UNVERIFIABLE as to identity of the two figures on current evidence.

The 40% denominator. If $203m is 40% of spend to date, spend to date was about $508m (computed), which is inconsistent with ~$1.57–2bn total and therefore implies the audit covered a sub-period or a single contractor rather than all project spending. Verification note: the draft audit's scope statement would resolve this.

  • Conventional construction reports were later revised; before revision they showed the project $28.1 million ahead of scheduled expenditures and $47.6 million over budget (2). [SRC: audit] VERIFIED
  • Voucher accounting reconciliation differences of $35.8 million (FY1993) and $12.4 million (FY1994) (2). [SRC: audit] VERIFIED
  • Funding for six Memorandum Purchase Orders rose by a total of $36.1 million without explanation (2). [SRC: audit] VERIFIED
  • A $1.2 million invoice had only a generic description (2). [SRC: audit] VERIFIED

  • Of 89 operative contract clauses, 25 deviated from standard provisions; two limited the contractor's liability (2). [SRC: audit] VERIFIED

The May 1993 computer-calculated estimate-at-completion showed a $124 million overrun, while an independent Departmental assessment reported an overrun of about $1.6 billion (4). [SRC: audit] VERIFIED. Verification note: this is one of the sharpest documented items in the file — the project's own control system and the Department's own reviewers differed by a factor of about 13 on the same project at the same date.

1.5 Consultants, perks and the Japanese contribution#

  • Consultants seeking a $500 million to $1.5 billion Japanese contribution were paid $1,000 a day plus $19,990 in expenses and collected $38,298 before cancellation; they expected fees of $15–20 million for a $1 billion contribution (2). [SRC: audit] VERIFIED
  • Foreign contributions never arrived. DOE projected about $2.6 billion from Texas and foreign contributors; OMB expected $1.8 billion from non-federal sources, about $900 million from Texas. Texas provided its requested funding; none of the seven countries approached provided cash. Canada's projected $25 million exceeded its entire annual high-energy physics budget; the UK's expected $15 million was never committed (2). As of December 1992, against a goal of about $1.7 billion, only about $15 million had been received (3). [SRC: audit] VERIFIED
  • Perks. The Inspector General examined "$500,000 in questionable expenses over three years," including about $12,000 for Christmas parties, $25,000 for catered lunches, $21,000 for office plants (5, citing Los Angeles Times, "Super-Collider Perks Under Investigation," 10 June 1993). [SRC: press] REPORTED. Verification note: the LA Times piece and the underlying IG workpapers would confirm; the LA Times article was not retrievable here.

[SRC: official] via press REPORTED

[SRC: official] via press REPORTED
  • Fee reductions: $1 million (2023) and more than $1 million (2024) (13). [SRC: official] via press REPORTED

  • Contractor replaced. In January 2025, Fermi Forward Discovery Group (FermiForward) — University of Chicago, Universities Research Association, Amentum Environment & Energy, Longenecker & Associates — replaced FRA as M&O contractor. Some observers were surprised the new group has a composition and leadership similar to FRA's (13). [SRC: press] REPORTED

2.4 SLAC — a clean incurred-cost audit with qualifications#

DOE-OIG-26-39, Stanford University's Costs Incurred and Claimed for Fiscal Years 2016, 2018, 2019 and 2020 Under Contract DE-AC02-76SF00515 (published June 2026), audit performed by CohnReznick LLP for the DOE OIG (9). [SRC: audit] VERIFIED

  • CohnReznick "did not identify any questioned costs." (9)
  • Approximately $272 million in subcontractor costs unresolved pending audit (9)
  • Two control deficiencies: a lack of documentation to support costs, and a lack of adequate controls over timesheets for certain labor costs (9)

7.2 The policy architecture#

CERN Policy on Support for Companies Established to Exploit CERN Technologies ("spin-off policy"), 3 August 2018 (45) [SRC: official] VERIFIED:

What CERN gives: "Access to CERN technologies, technical support, equipment and infrastructure under favourable conditions"; use of CERN labels; access to its incubator network and to its network of entrepreneur-minded individuals; exclusive licences limited to specific fields, limited duration and milestones; favourable financial terms for non-exclusive licences including a royalty grace period ("for instance one year during which no royalties on sales are due") and preferential rates for technical support.

What CERN does not do: "CERN does not invest nor does it take equity in spin-off companies"; "CERN does not provide any funding from its regular budget"; "CERN shall not be represented on boards or committees of spin-off companies"; spin-offs cannot be seated on the CERN site; technology cannot be transferred for military applications; support (except licensing) is time-limited and spin-offs are expected to become self-sustaining.

Staff interests: shareholding is permitted — "CERN personnel in principle are not forbidden from doing so, nor do they require authorisation" — subject to conflict-of-interest rules. Board or committee roles require prior authorisation of the Director-General for MPEs. "members of the KT Group cannot hold or receive any equity or other interest in a spin-off company."

Leave: full-time absence requires prior authorisation for Special Leave for professional reasons or use of the Saved Leave scheme; part-time options via Saved Leave or reduced hours; at contract end, special leave may be granted for "not more than 1 working day per week."

Conflict-of-interest safeguards: personnel must "take every possible measure to avoid such situations"; interests must be disclosed to the KT Group; inventors with a stake "are not entitled to initiate new work on that technology as part of their CERN duties"; procurement from spin-offs requires Procurement involvement during contract negotiation.

Verification note on a real gap: the policy permits unauthorised, undeclared-to-the-public shareholding by CERN personnel in spin-offs (disclosure runs to the KT Group, internally). There is no public register of CERN personnel interests in companies, and no published post-employment restriction ("cooling-off period"). CERN's Conflict of Interest Policy page was unavailable — the content is being archived into the CERN Library collections — so the policy text itself could not be read (see §12). On public evidence this is an undetectable-by-design condition rather than a finding of wrongdoing: there is no mechanism by which an outsider could identify a self-dealing case if one existed.

Structural note on the biggest gap. Across targets 5, 6 and 7 — procurement pricing, compensation, and the revolving door — the pattern is the same and it is not an accident of this search. CERN publishes its audited accounts and its budget, and has them certified annually by a rotating national supreme audit institution. It does not publish its internal audit output, its non-competitive award statistics, its senior remuneration, its personnel interest declarations, or any price benchmark for its single-source purchases. It is also immune from national jurisdiction, so no bid-protest record can exist. The consequence is that the conditions under which overcharging or self-dealing would be possible are documented — by CERN itself, in its own technical papers, which name the "high cost of sole supplier" — while the data that would show whether either occurred is, as a matter of structure rather than of concealment, not available to anyone outside the organisation. That is a finding about the evidence base, not about conduct, and the distinction should survive into the assessment phase.
