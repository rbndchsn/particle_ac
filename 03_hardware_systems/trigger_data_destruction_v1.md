## What this stream claims and what it does not

The argument under test has four joints. Stated precisely:

1. The LHC produces bunch crossings at 40 MHz and permanently records a rate three to four orders of magnitude below that.
2. The discarded events are discarded in real time, and at the first level the discarding is performed by fixed-latency hardware whose decision cannot be revisited.
3. The trigger menu - the enumerated set of signatures the system is built to keep - is constructed from theoretical expectation and Monte Carlo simulation.
4. Therefore the apparatus encodes a theoretical prior at the exact point where data loss becomes irreversible.

Joints 1, 2 and 3 are established in the collaborations' own technical documents, in the collaborations' own words, and are not in dispute. They are set out in the next three sections with numbers.

Joint 4 follows, but a fifth claim that the brief attached to it - that physics outside the menu is **unobservable in principle** rather than merely unmeasured - does **not** survive the evidence, and is retired in the closing section. The defensible replacement is quantitative: physics outside the menu is suppressed by a large, finite, and in-principle-calculable effective prescale, which for the fully unbiased component of LHC data is of order 40,000 (1). That is a weaker claim than "unobservable in principle" and a much stronger one than "merely unmeasured," because the suppression factor is a number the field can be asked to publish and has not.

## The rate hierarchy, quantified

**Bunch crossings and interactions.** The LHC crosses bunches every 25 ns, a rate of 40 MHz. The underlying proton-proton interaction rate is higher still: of order 10^9 Hz, with tens of interactions per crossing at design luminosity [A] (2).

**ATLAS.** The Level-1 (L1) trigger accepts events "up to the maximum detector read-out rate of 100 kHz," down from 40 MHz, with the decision made at a fixed latency below 2.5 microseconds [A] (3). At L1 accept the data rate is 300 GB/s for events of about 3 MB. In Run 2 the High-Level Trigger (HLT) wrote physics output at about 1.2 kHz and 1.2 GB/s [A] (4). For Run 3 the target average HLT physics output rate was raised to 3 kHz, against 1.2 kHz in Run 2, with event size compressed from about 3 MB to below 2 MB and bandwidth to permanent storage doubled to allow up to 8 GB/s [A] (3).

The resulting rejection factors for ATLAS Run 3, from the figures above: L1 about 400:1, HLT about 33:1, overall about 1.3 x 10^4:1. For Run 2, with 1.2 kHz output, overall about 3.3 x 10^4:1 [C - arithmetic on (3) and (4); the collaborations do not publish the product as a single figure].

**CMS.** The L1 output is capped at 100 kHz, described in the Run 1 trigger paper as "the upper limit imposed by the CMS readout electronics," with the L1 decision required within 4 microseconds [A] (5). In Run 1 the HLT selected an average of 400 Hz for storage, from events of about 0.5 MB [A] (5). In Run 2 the 2018 menu totalled about 1.6 kHz at a luminosity of 1.8 x 10^34 cm^-2 s^-1, rising to 1640 Hz of storage once a 6.6% dataset overlap is counted, from about 600 HLT paths for pp running [A] (6). In Run 3 L1 and HLT "operate at typical output rates of 110 kHz and 5 kHz, respectively," against an HLT originally designed to reduce the rate to "around 1 kHz before data storage" [A] (7).

**Aggregate.** Full readout of all LHC data would be about 40 TB/s, against the order of 1 GB/s actually written to permanent storage - about 1 part in 40,000 by volume [A] (8). A real-time-analysis review states the position without euphemism: the triggers "select roughly between 0.001% and 0.01% of the most interesting data collected by the detectors for further analysis and permanently discard the rest," a reduction between 1/1000 and 1/100000 [A] (9). Ellis puts the ceiling differently: "the maximum fraction of interactions that can be selected is O(10^-7)" [A] (2).

**Note on the brief's figure.** The brief's "roughly 1 kHz" is the Run 2 number, and it overstates present loss. Run 3 ATLAS targets 3 kHz; CMS Run 3 records roughly 2 kHz of standard physics, about 3 kHz of parked data, and 26-30 kHz of scouting data (7)(10). Counting all recorded streams, the Run 3 kept fraction is nearer 10^-3 than the 2.5 x 10^-5 implied by 1 kHz. The correction matters because the streams that make up the difference are precisely the mitigations, and they were built in response to this problem.

## Where the loss becomes irreversible, and in what sense

The field says it plainly. Ellis: "The efficiency for the desired physics must be kept high at all levels since **rejected events are lost forever**" [A] (2). The real-time-analysis review: the trigger "permanently discard[s] the rest," and at the hardware level data is held for a fixed window "after which the data is either read from the detector electronics or lost" [A] (9). A community review of hidden physics states the consequence for reanalysis: "only a subset data is stored and hence the lost data can never be recovered" [A] (11).

**The physical mechanism.** Front-end electronics retain "the information from each BC, for each detector element ... during the latency of the LVL1 trigger (several microseconds)" [A] (2). The ATLAS liquid-argon front-end boards provide "analog storage of the detector signals during the level-1 trigger latency of approximately 2.5 microseconds," with digitisation at 40 MHz and five 25 ns samples read out per accepted event [A] (12). The buffer is finite and cyclic: it must be reused every few microseconds, so a decision not taken within latency is a decision taken against the event. Dead-time is introduced deliberately "to prevent the derandomizers from overflowing," and ATLAS specified under 1% dead-time at 75 kHz and under 6% at 100 kHz [A] (2).

**Three distinct irreversibilities, which the argument should not conflate** [C - decomposition mine; rests on (2)(5)(9)(13)(14)]:

- *L1 rejection* is hardware, fixed-latency, and final. This is the only stage that is literally destruction by hardware within microseconds. Falsified if any ATLAS or CMS subsystem were shown to buffer beyond L1 latency in a way permitting retrospective recovery; no such capability is documented.
- *HLT rejection* is software and equally permanent as to the record, but it is not hardware destruction. It occurs at second-to-minute timescales on a CPU farm of roughly 30,500 cores at the end of Run 2, averaging 451 ms per event at pileup 50 [A] (6).
- *Deferred reconstruction* is not destruction at all. CMS parked the 2018 B-physics stream unprocessed to tape and reconstructed it between May and December 2019, against 48 hours for standard streams [A] (13). This shows the decision to *reconstruct* is deferrable even where the decision to *record* is not - a distinction the strong form of the argument tends to blur.

## The menu is a prior, in the field's own words

This is the load-bearing section, and it does not require inference.

Beck, writing on high-luminosity collider triggering: "The trigger menu defines the strategy of selecting events, **which are believed in advance of being interesting**." And: "**theoretical models and detailed Monte Carlo simulations guide the definition of the trigger menus**" [A] (14). He concedes the consequence: raising thresholds to fit bandwidth "can lead to missing a certain class of physics events," and "there is always room for the unexpected to escape detection, even in the most sophisticated trigger systems of today" [A] (14).

Ellis states the signature list directly - L1 triggers at hadron colliders search for high transverse momentum objects, isolated leptons, photons, and missing transverse energy [A] (2).

The hidden-physics review states the circularity explicitly: a dedicated trigger "necessarily needs physicists to know beforehand what kind of non-standard signature is expected," and the consequence is that "**only theories that the field already knows are visible in standard triggers**" [A] (11).

CMS states it in the motivation for its own anomaly-detection trigger: the conventional approach "risks missing unexpected new physics signatures not anticipated by predefined selection criteria," and "this reliance on specific criteria introduces the risk of bias" [A] (15).

The Snowmass 2021 Instrumentation Frontier report: hadron-collider triggers select high-momentum calorimeter objects and this "necessarily loses many events"; dark matter "might be missed by the existing trigger systems"; and the report calls for moving beyond "fixed hand-curated trigger menus" [A] (16).

**The prior has a published numerical shape.** The 2018 CMS HLT thresholds state it: single isolated muon 24 GeV, single isolated electron 32 GeV, single photon 200 GeV, single particle-flow jet 500 GeV, missing transverse momentum 120 GeV, total hadronic activity H_T 1050 GeV [A] (6). In ATLAS Run 2 the lowest unprescaled single-jet trigger required p_T above 420 GeV at the HLT, with prescaled chains reaching down to 45 GeV [A] (17). Beck notes that a 20 GeV single-jet L1 chain requires "a huge pre-scale value of 100 000" to fit a 1.7 kHz budget [A] (14).

**Who decides, and how often.** ATLAS Run 2: the pp menu held "approximately 1500 individual event selections," specifying signatures, algorithms, and the allocated event rate and bandwidth; it "must reflect the physics goals for a given data collection period"; "the composition of the trigger menu is developed based on the expected luminosity for each year," with annual revision for 2016 and 2017 and the 2018 menu containing only additions on top of 2017 [A] (4). The configuration "is driven by the physics priorities of the experiment, including the number of clients satisfied by a particular trigger chain," with an on-call team responsible for active triggers and prescales under two operation coordinators [A] (4). CMS deploys roughly a dozen menus per year for pp running, and attributes HLT rate to physics groups as Total, Pure and Shared: roughly one third of the rate to beyond-Standard-Model searches, one third to measurements including the Higgs, and one third to B physics and object groups [A] (6).

That one-third/one-third/one-third split is the clearest single artefact in this stream. It is a budget allocation over hypothesis classes, decided annually by committee, implemented in firmware, and applied at the point where loss is irreversible.

## Signatures systematically disfavoured, with numbers

**Low mass and soft.** CMS's own review of scouting and parking states the problem in its first pages: "intriguing signal events characterized by lower energy and momenta may inadvertently be discarded," and the low-mass dijet regime "had become inaccessible" under tightened trigger selections [A] (10). Quantitatively: the standard CMS trigger "becomes fully efficient only for m_jj > 1.25 TeV," against a scouting plateau near 500 GeV; standard H_T triggers require H_T of about 1000 GeV or more, against about 300 GeV for scouting; and standard dimuon triggers "show significant acceptance losses below about 40 GeV," against scouting reach down to about 210 MeV [A] (10).

**Long-lived and displaced.** The CERN LPCC Long-Lived Particles Working Group review, produced with ATLAS and CMS trigger expertise, finds: models "can easily evade H_T or jet-based triggers"; models with displaced taus "are currently nearly entirely unprobed"; "while ATLAS has triggers that target decays in the calorimeters or in the muon system, there is currently no dedicated trigger for decays in the ID"; in Run 2 "ATLAS did not have corresponding di-lepton triggers targeting displaced light leptons"; and "pointing assumptions at L1 are a major limiting factor for the efficiency of L1 triggers for displaced muons" [A] (18). For LHCb downstream-track LLPs with no accompanying long track, "the expected trigger efficiencies are below few %" [A] (18).

**Quantified gains available from relaxing the prior** [A] (18): lowering the H_T cut from 500 GeV to 150 GeV yields "a factor of 10 increase in signal" for a 20 GeV pseudoscalar; a CMS muon track-finder change "improves the acceptance for larger lifetimes by as much as a factor of two"; LHCb tau trigger efficiency was "set to increase from 17% in Run 1 to 64% in Run 3."

**High-multiplicity soft and unconventional.** Di Petrillo and co-authors quantify signature by signature: for soft unclustered energy patterns, track p_T thresholds at or above 2 GeV give "negligible efficiency" for all mediator masses, while 0.5 GeV with a 100-track requirement reaches 50% above 200 GeV mediator mass; Higgs-portal two-track triggers fall below 20% efficiency for all scalar masses once thresholds exceed 2 GeV; long-lived stau acceptance times efficiency rises from about 0% to about 20% when the impact-parameter endpoint is extended from 10 mm to 100 mm [A] (19). Their framing is the pragmatist's: "Triggering on 20% of events is infinitely better than triggering on none" [A] (19).

**Displaced Higgs.** Buckley, Halyo and Lujan find that for displaced Higgs decays "many events pass the L1 trigger, but in a standard analysis the requirement to pass the HLT reduces the efficiency by nearly a factor of 50," attributing the loss "primarily ... to the high H_T requirement"; combining all available HLTs could reach about 5% efficiency but is "prohibitively impractical" [B - simulation study, not a collaboration measurement] (20).

**A documented case where the trigger prevented a measurement outright.** CMS states that it lacked a trigger for B to K(*) e+e-, which "has thus far prevented the measurements of R_K and R_K* by the CMS Collaboration" [A] (13). This is the cleanest instance in the literature of the menu, not the detector or the luminosity, being the binding constraint on a specific measurement - and the remedy was a deliberate menu change: a single-displaced-muon parking strategy delivering about 2 kHz averaged over a fill (about 2 GB/s), peaking at 50 kHz at L1 and 5.4 kHz at HLT, yielding about 10 billion unbiased b hadron decays in 7.6 PB of single-copy tape [A] (13).

**A documented case where an unanticipated phenomenon was recorded only because someone built a trigger for it.** The CMS ridge - long-range near-side angular correlations in high-multiplicity pp collisions - was observed with "a dedicated high multiplicity trigger ... designed and implemented in the two levels of the CMS trigger system": an L1 seed requiring total calorimeter transverse energy above 60 GeV, and an HLT requirement on online pixel tracks of N > 70, later raised to 85 [A] (21). The paper reports that "the statistics of events with N_trk_offline >= 110 were enhanced by a factor of about 1000 with the high multiplicity trigger relative to the minimum bias trigger," and that "the minimum bias trigger was heavily prescaled during higher luminosity LHC running" [A] (21). The effect was unexpected: it "has never been seen in dihadron correlation measurements of pp collisions," and such events occur "with a probability of only 10^-5 to 10^-6" [A] (22).

The ridge is the argument's best case and its sharpest limit at once. Best case: an unanticipated phenomenon required a purpose-built trigger, and the purpose-built trigger existed because someone anticipated the *observable* (multiplicity) even without anticipating the *effect*. Sharpest limit: the minimum-bias stream did contain such events, at roughly 1/1000 the statistics. The physics was not unobservable. It was 1000 times harder. [C - this reading is mine; it rests on the factor-1000 enhancement and the prescale statement in (21), and would be falsified by a demonstration that the min-bias sample could not in principle have reached discovery significance.]

## Mitigations and their real limits

**CMS scouting.** Reduced-content events of about 8.9 kB (calorimeter) to 14.8 kB (particle flow), roughly a hundred times smaller than standard events of about 0.86-0.87 MB, recorded at about 5 kHz in 2018 and about 30 kHz in 2022 and 26 kHz in 2023 [A] (10)(7). Limits, stated by CMS: "No raw data from detector channels are stored for later offline analysis"; "since there is no offline reconstruction in the scouting streams" the content is limited to HLT objects; simplified HLT tracking "reduces the efficiency and resolution of track parameters"; and b-jet tagging and reconstructed tau leptons "ha[ve] not been employed so far in scouting-based analyses" [A] (10).

**ATLAS trigger-level analysis.** Up to 27 kHz at a mean 6.5 kB per event, against 1.2 kHz of full 1 MB events in the main stream [A] (17). It delivered the dijet search region 375-1800 GeV, against conventional ATLAS Run 2 dijet searches with a minimum mass of 1.5 TeV, and extended the prior TLA floor from 450 GeV and the search region "from 600 GeV to 375 GeV" [A] (17). Limits, stated by ATLAS: "No tracking measurements were recorded for TLA"; "vertex information is not recorded for the TLA stream," so the standard pileup correction could not be applied; and the stream stored HLT jet information "while discarding the raw detector data found in the main stream" [A] (17).

**The mitigations relocate the irreversibility rather than removing it** [C - rests on the limits quoted above from (10) and (17)]. Scouting and TLA buy back a region of kinematic phase space by permanently discarding the detector-level record for every event in that region. The recovered data cannot be re-reconstructed with improved calibration, cannot be reinterpreted with a tracking-based observable nobody had thought of in 2018, and cannot support a b-tag or tau that was not computed online. The first-order destruction (which events) is traded for a second-order destruction (which observables, for the events kept). This would be falsified if any scouting or TLA dataset were shown to retain sufficient raw information for a full offline re-reconstruction; the collaborations state it does not.

**CMS parking.** Transferred "unprocessed, to tape storage" and reconstructed when resources allow: 400 Hz in 2012, 500 Hz in 2016, 400 Hz in 2017, 3000 Hz in 2018 [A] (10). This is the one mitigation that preserves the full record, and it is bounded by tape and by offline CPU rather than by L1 bandwidth.

**LHCb's removal of the hardware trigger.** The strongest institutional admission in the field. LHCb identified "the main limitation coming from the maximum allowed output rate of the first trigger stage, the L0, implemented in hardware," found that simple inclusive L0 selections "would result in an effective loss of efficiency with increasing luminosity," and concluded that "profiting from a higher luminosity to collect significantly more data is therefore only possible by removing the L0 trigger stage" [A] (23). Run 3 LHCb reads out at the visible interaction rate of about 30 MHz with no hardware trigger, at about 4 TB/s, with HLT1 reducing to about 1 MHz and HLT2 to about 20 kHz, writing about 10 GB/s to disk [A] (8)(24). Sources disagree on the HLT2 figure: one comparative review gives about 100 kHz (8); a CERN Courier account gives 20 kHz (24). Flagged, unresolved.

**ALICE's continuous readout.** Several Run 3 subdetectors read out continuously, and the lower-level trigger "applies no selection"; raw data is buffered in a 100 PB store with the full chain taking about three weeks; pp at 1 MHz, p-Pb at 500 kHz, Pb-Pb at 50 kHz, with HLT processing up to 48 GB/s and output up to 12 GB/s [A] (8)(24). ALICE and LHCb together are described as giving "essentially triggerless access to all events" [A] (24).

**Zero-bias and enhanced-bias streams.** ATLAS records an enhanced-bias dataset including "a sample collected by triggering on random filled bunches" to supply the zero-bias component, at about 300 Hz for roughly one hour, giving about one million events; and a minimum-bias trigger for luminosity calibration at about 5 kHz on five bunches, reading out inner-detector data only [A] (4).

**Pileup as a zero-bias sample.** Nachman and Rubbo observe that "every event passing any trigger can be used for the purpose of studying the pileup interactions, which are recorded with nearly no selection bias," and compute the resulting suppression: "the effective prescale for this Zero Bias Dataset is about 40000 in Runs 2+3, and drops to 400 for trigger-level analyses," accumulating 0.5-1 fb^-1 of fully unbiased pp collision data by the end of HL-LHC operations and supporting cross-section limits of "nearly 3 fb" for models with near-zero Standard Model background [A] (1). They open from the same premise as this stream - "most events produced at the Large Hadron Collider (LHC) are discarded in real time," and "there are a plethora of viable models of physics beyond the Standard Model (BSM) that are not well covered" - and arrive at a recovery mechanism rather than a foreclosure [B] (1).

**Anomaly detection in firmware.** CMS's AXOL1TL deploys a variational autoencoder encoder on Xilinx Virtex-7 FPGAs in the Global Trigger, with the anomaly score approximated by the sum of squared latent means, commissioned in 2024 data-taking; it reports a "46% efficiency gain when compared to rest of L1 trigger" at 1 kHz for exotic Higgs decays to four b quarks [A] (15). Two caveats, both in the paper: the 46% figure is described only as "estimated," and the work was run in a Global Trigger test crate whose output "is not used to trigger the readout of CMS" [A] (15). **A background-trained autoencoder replaces a signal prior with a background prior; it does not eliminate prior-dependence** [C - rests on the training description in (15); falsified by a trigger whose selection is demonstrably independent of any learned or assumed distribution, which does not exist].

**HL-LHC direction of travel.** The first-level hardware trigger rate rises "from the current 100 kHz to 1 MHz in ATLAS and 760 kHz in CMS," with latency rising to 10 microseconds in ATLAS and 12.5 in CMS [A] (24). CMS projects total disk needs "approach[ing] 1/2 exabyte" by 2030 and about 150 billion events processed per year, and notes that with fewer resources it would compensate by "increasing physics thresholds e.g. for reconstruction algorithms, and thus initially compromising physics sensitivities" [A] (25).

## The counterargument, at full strength

The strongest version of the defence has four parts, and three of them hold.

**(a) The menu is deliberately broad and deliberately inclusive.** Beck: "trigger menus usually contain a mixture of exclusive and inclusive trigger chains," and "inclusive trigger chains at relatively low energy and momentum thresholds need to be maintained" because "the unexpected shall not be lost" [A] (14). ATLAS Run 3 strategy "gives most of the rate to inclusive triggers," with extra HLT rate going to expanding the physics menu [A] (3). Karaca's study of ATLAS menu design identifies general-purpose items added for unforeseen processes rather than specific predictions - missing-energy, total-energy and jet-energy thresholds with no model attached - alongside prescaled low-threshold triggers added because higher LHC rates "raise the chance of detecting novel processes" [A] (26). Ellis records that discovery searches explicitly aim at "new physics that has not been predicted (by using inclusive signatures)" [A] (2).

**(b) The menu is revised in response to theory, and demonstrably so.** The CMS B-parking programme exists because a lepton-flavour-universality tension existed elsewhere [A] (13). The LPCC review is a standing institutional mechanism for theorists to lodge trigger requests, and its recommendations entered Run 3 menus [A] (18). The hidden-physics review credits exactly this: "discussions between experimentalists and theorists led to the development of new triggers, new external detectors, and influenced the planning of future experiments" [A] (11). Menus are revised annually (4)(6).

**(c) There is a named, published counter-position on the epistemology.** Karaca argues that LHC data selection "is theory-laden in that its implementation is crucially dependent on the theoretical models of high energy particle physics," but that it "is not driven by the above-mentioned theoretical models, but rather by a particular data selection strategy" - his term is strategy-drivenness. His conclusion inverts the critical reading: the procedure "opens up otherwise unavailable possibilities for the ATLAS experiment to fulfill the entire range of its objectives," and "theory-ladenness plays an essential role in experimentation being exploratory" [B] (26). He concedes the cost - without an appropriate menu "the data selection procedure would be biased against certain types of interesting events," and "only a minute fraction of the interesting events could be acquired for evaluation" - but reads the menu as an instrument of exploration rather than of foreclosure [B] (26).

This is the argument's most serious opposition and it must be met on its own ground. Karaca is right that no single model determines the menu. The critical claim survives in weaker form: a selection strategy is still a prior over signature space, and strategy-drivenness changes the prior's provenance from "a theory" to "the field's collective sense of what an interesting event looks like." That is a prior with no owner, which is harder to audit than one derived from a named model, not easier.

**(d) The bandwidth constraint is a hard limit rather than a choice.** This part is **partly true and partly not**, and the distinction is where the argument has its best remaining purchase.

*Genuinely physical:* for ATLAS and CMS "the readout cables for the subdetectors have to run through the detector volume," so cable mass must be minimised to avoid scattering particles - a constraint on off-detector bandwidth imposed by the physics the detector is there to measure [A] (9). Front-end buffer depth is bounded by the same radiation-hard, low-mass, in-volume electronics budget (12).

*Genuinely economic:* storing all LHCb data "would cost around 21,000 times more" than real-time analysis first, against data storage and processing costs of "around 7 million CHF per year" [A] (9); and CMS's HL-LHC plan is explicitly "build-to-cost" (25).

*Historically contingent:* "the technology to transfer such data rates simply wasn't available" when the detectors were designed, and "economically transferring Terabytes of data per second is only just becoming possible" [A] (9). And the decisive evidence against treating the limit as a law: LHCb removed its hardware trigger entirely, ALICE applies no selection at the lower-level trigger and buffers 100 PB, and future experiments including CBM at FAIR and ePIC at the EIC "aim for 'triggerless' operation" [A] (23)(8)(24).

**The honest statement is therefore:** the bandwidth constraint binding ATLAS and CMS is real, is partly physical, and is not a law of nature. Two of the four LHC experiments abandoned the hardware trigger that the argument treats as inevitable, and the field's own roadmap calls for moving "away from a pipelined and triggered readout" toward streaming designs "pioneered by LHCb" [A] (16). The prior is encoded at the point of irreversible loss because ATLAS and CMS chose a two-level architecture and kept it through the HL-LHC upgrade, not because triggering as such requires it.

## The astrophysical comparison, which does not support the analogy

The brief asked whether the same problem appears in astroparticle detectors. It does not, and the reason is instructive.

**IceCube.** Merged trigger rate 2.5-2.9 kHz, median 2.7 kHz, producing about 1 TB/day of raw DAQ output against a satellite allocation of "about 100 GB/day" over roughly 12 hours of geosynchronous connectivity at 250 Mbps; about 25 filter selections operate each year, "set by the collaboration each year" and "tuned to specific analyses," selecting about 15% of triggered events [A] (27). But: "raw files contain all uncalibrated waveforms from all DOMs for every event," the raw archive is the full about 1 TB/day, and archival data are stored locally on disks in two copies and physically transported to the IceCube data centre each year [A] (27).

**That is the whole difference.** IceCube's filtering is a bandwidth-allocation problem on a satellite link, not a destruction problem. The prior encoded in the 25 annual filters is fully revisable because the raw record survives on disks that fly north. A filter choice made in 2016 can be undone in 2026.

**Pierre Auger.** Rejection from about 3 kHz per tank to about 3 events per day, a factor of 10^8 - a steeper reduction than the LHC's [A] (28). T1 time-over-threshold about 1.6 Hz per station, T1 threshold about 100 Hz, T2 about 20 Hz to the central station, T3 about 600 events per day from the 3ToT condition and about 400 per day from the 4-fold condition, of which only 2% are real showers; full efficiency above 10^19 eV; the physics T4 loses under 5% of showers below 60 degrees zenith [A] (28). The trigger was designed "while keeping the rate constraints imposed by the communication and data acquisition system" [A] (28).

**Telescope Array.** Level-0 at about 750 Hz per detector (0.3 MIP) stored in an on-board memory buffer; Level-1 at about 30 Hz (3.0 MIP) sent over a 2.4 GHz wireless LAN whose "band rate of the data acquisition is approximately 1 Mbps"; Level-2 formed when "three adjacent SDs are coincident within 8 microseconds," after which full waveforms are collected within plus or minus 32 microseconds of trigger time; trigger efficiency reaches about 100% above 10^19 eV [A] (29).

**Conclusion** [C - the comparison and its consequence are mine; rests on (27)(28)(29) against (2)(9)(11)]: the astroparticle detectors share the rate-reduction architecture and the bandwidth-driven prior, but not the irreversibility. IceCube preserves the full raw record; Auger and Telescope Array buffer locally and lose only sub-threshold or non-coincident data. So the LHC's irreversibility is a consequence of a particular rate-and-volume regime and a particular architectural choice, not a necessary feature of triggered experiments. This **strengthens** the specificity of the irreversibility claim and **removes** any appeal to "everyone has this problem" as a defence. It would be falsified by evidence that IceCube's raw archive is incomplete or not in fact retained.

## Has the discovery-space loss been quantified?

Partially, and never integrated.

What exists [A]: the effective prescale on the fully unbiased component, about 40,000 for Runs 2 and 3 and about 400 at trigger-level-analysis granularity, with the resulting 0.5-1 fb^-1 and about 3 fb limits (1); signature-by-signature acceptance curves for soft unclustered energy patterns, Higgs-portal scalars and long-lived staus (19); the H_T 500-to-150 GeV factor of 10 and the lifetime-acceptance factor of 2 (18); the 46% L1 efficiency gain for Higgs to four b quarks from an anomaly trigger (15); the dijet turn-on gap of 1.25 TeV standard against 500 GeV scouting, and 1.5 TeV conventional against 375 GeV trigger-level (10)(17); the factor-50 HLT efficiency penalty for displaced Higgs in simulation (20).

What does not exist: any published measure over signature space as a whole - no acceptance map, no coverage integral, no estimate of the L1 false-negative rate under any stated prior over non-Standard-Model signatures.

**The asymmetry of trigger error is unaudited** [C - rests on (2)(4)(6) plus the absence of any such publication across the sources surveyed]. A trigger false positive costs bandwidth, storage and CPU, all of which are measured, budgeted annually, and reported. A trigger false negative costs an unrecoverable event, is not measured, is not budgeted, and is reported only for signatures somebody thought to simulate. The collaborations publish efficiency for signatures in the menu; nobody publishes inefficiency integrated over signatures not in it, because that integral requires a prior over signature space that no committee will own. This is falsifiable in the most direct way available: it would be refuted by a single ATLAS, CMS or LPCC document presenting a signature-space coverage integral with a stated measure. The Snowmass Instrumentation Frontier report, asked precisely this question, "gives no quantified estimate of physics lost to existing triggers" (16).

**The structural consequence** [C - rests on the circularity quoted at (11), the menu revision cadence at (4) and (6), and the irreversibility at (2)(9)]: if a dedicated trigger "necessarily needs physicists to know beforehand what kind of non-standard signature is expected" (11), and menus are revised on an annual cadence (4)(6), and L1 rejection is final (2), then the set of signatures discoverable in data collected in year *t* is bounded by the set of signatures conceived before the year-*t* menu was frozen. The bound is not absolute - the zero-bias, enhanced-bias, minimum-bias, parked and pileup components leak through it, which is exactly why those components are the field's answer to this problem. But the bound is real, it has a timescale of about one year, and its width has never been measured. It would be falsified by a discovery made in a signature absent from every menu at collection time; the ridge is the nearest approach, and it required an observable (multiplicity) that someone had already decided to trigger on.

## Claims Examined and Not Supported

Tier D. Each of these was in the brief, or is a natural strengthening of it, and each failed against the evidence.

**1. "Physics outside the menu is unobservable in principle rather than merely unmeasured." Not supported. Must be softened.** The LHC records several streams with little or no signature prior: ATLAS enhanced-bias data including random triggers on filled bunches, about 300 Hz for an hour and about a million events (4); minimum-bias triggers (4)(21); prescaled low-threshold chains reaching 45 GeV in jets (17); and, decisively, the pileup interactions in every recorded event, "recorded with nearly no selection bias," carrying an effective prescale of about 40,000 and reaching 0.5-1 fb^-1 of fully unbiased data with about 3 fb cross-section sensitivity (1). The ridge supplies the empirical disproof: the minimum-bias stream contained the events, at about 1/1000 the statistics (21). **"In principle" is the wrong modality.** The defensible form: *physics outside the menu is suppressed by a large, finite and calculable effective prescale - of order 10^4 for the unbiased component - and is unobservable only where the product of cross-section, unbiased effective luminosity and detector acceptance falls below the statistical floor of that sample.* That is a quantitative boundary, it can be computed for any named model, and it is a far more useful claim than the categorical one because it can be checked.

**2. "The LHC records roughly 1 kHz." Out of date and understates what is kept.** 1 kHz is ATLAS and CMS Run 2 (4)(6). ATLAS Run 3 targets 3 kHz (3); CMS Run 3 runs L1 at about 110 kHz with roughly 2 kHz standard, about 3 kHz parked and 26-30 kHz scouting (7)(10). Counting all recorded streams the Run 3 kept fraction is nearer 10^-3 than 2.5 x 10^-5. Using the 1 kHz figure overstates present loss by between 3x and 30x depending on the stream accounting, and it silently omits the mitigations - which is self-defeating, since those mitigations are the field's acknowledgement of the problem.

**3. "The discarded events are destroyed in real time by hardware." True only of Level-1.** L1 is hardware (FPGA and ASIC), fixed-latency, and final: under 2.5 microseconds in ATLAS, about 4 in CMS, bounded by finite cyclic front-end buffers (3)(5)(12). HLT rejection is software, at 451 ms per event on a 30,500-core farm (6) - permanent as to the record, but not hardware destruction, and reached with full event information in hand. And deferred reconstruction is not destruction at all: CMS parked 7.6 PB unprocessed and reconstructed it 6-13 months later (13). The claim should be stated at three levels, not one.

**4. "No one has quantified the discovery-space loss." Not supported as an absolute.** Partial quantifications exist and are listed above (1)(15)(18)(19)(20). The accurate claim is narrower and still strong: *no integrated measure over signature space has been published, and the Snowmass Instrumentation Frontier report, addressing exactly this, offers no quantified estimate* (16).

**5. "The same problem appears in astrophysical detectors." Not supported - the analogy fails on the only point that matters.** IceCube archives all uncalibrated waveforms from all DOMs for every event, about 1 TB/day, in two local copies shipped north annually, against a 100 GB/day satellite link (27). Auger and Telescope Array buffer locally and lose only sub-threshold or non-coincident signals (28)(29). These are reversible filtering regimes. Invoking them as parallel cases weakens the LHC argument by implying the irreversibility is generic; it is not, and the contrast is better used the other way round.

**6. "The bandwidth constraint is a hard physical and financial limit rather than a choice." Partly supported, and cannot be used as a full defence.** Physical: readout cable mass must be minimised because the cables traverse the tracking volume (9). Financial: 21,000x cost multiple for full LHCb storage, about 7 MCHF/yr, build-to-cost HL-LHC planning approaching half an exabyte of disk (9)(25). But LHCb removed its hardware trigger because that trigger was the binding physics limitation (23), ALICE applies no selection at the lower-level trigger and buffers 100 PB (8)(24), and the field's own roadmap calls for streaming readout (16). The constraint is architectural and era-specific. Two of four LHC experiments did not accept it.

**7. "The field does not acknowledge this." Clearly false, and the claim would discredit the stream if made.** Acknowledgement is extensive, institutional, and in the collaborations' own documents: a Physics Reports review whose entire premise is that standard triggers discard interesting low-energy events (10); a standing CERN LPCC working group enumerating uncovered long-lived-particle signatures (18); a Snowmass community report stating that current triggers "necessarily lose many events" and calling for an end to "fixed hand-curated trigger menus" (16); CMS deploying anomaly-detection firmware explicitly because predefined criteria risk "missing unexpected new physics signatures" (15); and a community review stating that "only theories that the field already knows are visible in standard triggers" (11). The defensible critical claim is not that the field is unaware. It is that **the acknowledgement is distributed across dozens of signature-specific documents and has never been assembled into a single accounting of what the apparatus cannot see** - and that the one quantity which would make the problem auditable, an acceptance measure over signature space, has no owner and no publication.

**8. "The trigger menu is designed from theoretical expectation." Supported, but incomplete as stated, and there is a named opponent.** Beck is unambiguous that models and Monte Carlo guide menu definition (14). But menus also serve detector calibration, luminosity measurement, and deliberate redundancy so that efficiency can be measured from data (2)(4), and rate is allocated by committee across broad physics-group budgets rather than derived from models (6). Karaca's published position - that the procedure is theory-laden but strategy-driven, and that this is what makes the experiment exploratory rather than foreclosed - is the serious opposition and must be cited whenever this claim is made (26). The claim survives in the form: *the menu encodes a prior over signature space whose provenance is the field's collective expectation rather than any single model, which makes it harder to audit, not easier.*

**9. "Anomaly-detection triggers solve the prior problem." Not supported.** AXOL1TL's autoencoder is trained on background, so it encodes a background prior in place of a signal prior (15). Its headline 46% gain is "estimated," and the reported deployment ran in a Global Trigger test crate whose output "is not used to trigger the readout of CMS" (15). It is a genuine and important shift in where the prior sits. It is not an elimination of prior-dependence, and no trigger architecture that eliminates prior-dependence exists.

**10. "Matt Strassler documented a case where an LHC trigger threw away real physics." Not supported.** The widely cited "Trigger Failure, and Repair" piece argues from a hypothetical - Higgs decays to long-lived particles that "would be lost forever, using standard methods" - and reports that ATLAS subsequently built the triggers and set limits with no discovery (30). It is a clear exposition of the mechanism and a legitimate illustration, but it is not a documented instance of lost physics, and citing it as one is a factual error. The same caution applies to the displaced-Higgs factor-of-50 result, which is a simulation study (20). The genuinely documented instances are narrower and better: CMS's absent B to K(*) ee trigger preventing an R_K measurement outright (13), and the ridge's thousand-fold dependence on a purpose-built trigger (21).
